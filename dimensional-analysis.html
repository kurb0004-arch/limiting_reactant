How dimensional analysis works

Also called the factor-label method. You solve a conversion problem by following the units, starting with one conversion factor and building up to three. All of the factors use metric prefixes.

    The big idea

    A conversion factor is a fraction that equals 1, because the top and bottom describe the same amount. For example, 1000 mL and 1 L are the same volume, so 1000 mL ÷ 1 L = 1. Multiplying by 1 changes how a quantity is written, not how much of it there is.

    Units behave like numbers in a fraction: the same unit on the top and bottom cancels.

    1. Write what you start with, as a number and a unit.
    2. Pick a factor with the unit you want to remove on the bottom.
    3. Repeat until only the unit you want is left.
    4. Multiply the numbers on top and divide by the numbers on the bottom.

    Metric prefixes

    A prefix tells you how a unit compares to the base unit (meter, gram, or liter). Each row gives an equality you can turn into a conversion factor, shown here with meters.

    PrefixSymbolMeaningEquality
    kilok10001 km = 1000 m
    decid1/1010 dm = 1 m
    centic1/100100 cm = 1 m
    millim1/10001000 mm = 1 m
    microμ1/1,000,0001×10⁶ μm = 1 m
    nanon1/1,000,000,0001×10⁹ nm = 1 m

    The base unit is your bridge. To go from one prefix to another, go to the base unit first, then to the prefix you want.

    Level 1: one conversion factor

    Use one factor when your starting unit and your goal unit are directly related. Convert 2.50 L to milliliters. Step through it.

    Why the orientation matters

    Both fractions equal 1, but only one cancels the unit you want to remove.

    In a rate, a unit on the bottom of what you have cancels with the same unit on the top of a factor. Cross out units from both the top and the bottom as you go.

    Practice

    Common mistakes

    • Flipping a factor. If the units don’t cancel, flip it.
    • Dropping units partway. Carry every unit through every step and cross them out as they cancel.
    • Reversing a prefix. 1000 mm = 1 m, not 1 mm = 1000 m. The prefix goes with the bigger number.
    • Forgetting the base unit. Going from μg to mg is easier through grams than by guessing a single factor.
    • Rounding too early. Keep extra digits until the last step.
