How dimensional analysis works

Also called the factor-label method. It lets you solve conversion problems by following the units, so you always know what to multiply and what to divide.

The big idea

A conversion factor is a fraction that equals 1, because the top and bottom describe the same amount. For example, 1000 mL and 1 L are the same volume, so

1000 mL1 L=1

Multiplying by 1 changes how a quantity is written, not how much of it there is. Units behave like numbers in a fraction: the same unit on the top and bottom cancels.

The method:

  1. Write what you start with, as a number and a unit.
  2. Decide what unit you want to end with.
  3. Multiply by a conversion factor with the unit you want to get rid of on the bottom.
  4. Repeat until only the unit you want is left.
  5. Multiply the numbers on top, divide by the numbers on the bottom.

Why the orientation of the factor matters

Both fractions below equal 1, but only one of them cancels the unit you want to remove. Always put the unit you’re getting rid of on the bottom.

Example 2: grams to moles

How many moles are in 4.50 g of NaCl? The molar mass of NaCl is 58.44 g/mol, which gives the conversion factor 1 mol NaCl per 58.44 g NaCl.

Example 3: a long chain

This is the same kind of problem as a limiting reagent calculation. For the reaction 10 KClO₃ + 3 P₄ → 3 P₄O₁₀ + 10 KCl, how many grams of P₄O₁₀ can 55.0 g of KClO₃ make? Each factor moves one step: grams to moles, one substance to another, then moles to grams.

Always write the formula after every unit (mol KClO₃, not just mol). It tells you which substance each number belongs to, and it’s the easiest way to catch the mole-ratio mistake.

Common mistakes

  • Flipping a factor. If the units don’t cancel, flip it.
  • Dropping units partway. Carry every unit through every step, and cross them out as they cancel.
  • Mixing up substances. The mole ratio comes from the balanced equation and links two different substances. Grams of one are never converted straight into grams of another.
  • Using an unbalanced equation. The coefficients in your mole ratio must come from the balanced equation.
  • Rounding too early. Keep extra digits until the last step.
